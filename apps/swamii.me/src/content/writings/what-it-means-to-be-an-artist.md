## When the Tools Become Ubiquitous

---

I watched my skillset collapse.

Code—the thing I spent years mastering—became a commodity overnight. What took me months to learn, AI can do in seconds. The floor dropped out.

And I'm not alone. Engineers, designers, writers, musicians—everyone who built their identity on technical skill is feeling this. The tools we spent careers mastering are now available to everyone. With a prompt.

So what's left?

What makes you an artist when everyone has the same brushes?

---

## The Collapse

It happened fast. One year, "learn to code" was career advice. The next, code generation was free. Companies that needed teams of engineers suddenly needed one person with good taste and an AI assistant.

The skillset collapsed. Not gradually. All at once.

I felt it personally. I'm an engineer. I write code. Or—I did. Now I describe what I want and watch it appear. The craft I knew is gone.

Or is it?

---

## Code Was Always Expression

Here's what I realized: **code is expression**. Ideas are expression. The medium changed, but the essence didn't.

When painters got pre-mixed paint in tubes, it didn't make painting less of an art. When writers got word processors, it didn't make writing less of a craft. The tool changed. The work remained.

Programming was never about syntax. It was about **seeing systems**. Understanding problems. Building solutions that serve human needs. That didn't go away. It just stopped hiding behind the mechanics of semicolons and bracket matching.

The question isn't whether code is still an art form. It's whether we're still artists.

---

## What Artists Do

An artist puts effort into their work. Takes pride in what they do. Spends time—real time—shaping something into existence.

**The time spent on a project is what determines its output.** No matter what tools you use.

AI doesn't reduce the time you invest. It changes **where** you spend that time.

Less time fighting syntax errors. More time thinking about what you're building and why. Less time on mechanical execution. More time on vision, taste, judgment.

The craft deepens. It doesn't disappear.

---

## The Seven-League Boots

In fairy tales, seven-league boots let you walk 21 kilometers with a single step. You cover vast distances without exhaustion.

But here's the thing: **the boots don't tell you where to go.**

You still choose the direction. You still walk the path. The journey is still yours.

That's what technology is. Seven-league boots.

AI gives us reach we never had before. An artisan can build things that would have taken a team. A writer can explore ideas that would have taken years. A musician can compose symphonies alone.

But the boots don't create the destination. They don't replace the human walking.

They amplify. They extend. They serve.

The artist still decides where to walk.

---

## The Two Paths

When the floor drops out, you have two choices:

**Path One: Resist.**

Ban the new tools. Preserve the scarcity of skill. Insist that "real art" requires suffering through the old way.

This is the Luddite position. It's understandable. It's also futile.

Every generation of artists faces this. Painters fought photography. Illustrators fought Photoshop. Musicians fought synthesizers.

Art didn't die. It evolved. The ones who adopted the new tools early created entirely new forms. The ones who resisted got left behind—or found their niche in preserving the old ways, which is also valid, but smaller.

**Path Two: Adopt.**

Use the new tools. Focus on what tools can't do: vision, taste, meaning.

**Use them for good before anyone else can use them iniquitously.**

This is the Buckminster Fuller position. Don't fight the existing reality. Build a new model that makes the old one obsolete.

Take the seven-league boots. Walk somewhere worth going.

---

## What Remains Human

When everyone has the same tools, what differentiates an artist?

Not technical skill. That's commoditized.

What remains is **irreducibly human**:

- **Taste.** Knowing what's good. What matters. What resonates.
- **Judgment.** Choosing what to build. What to say no to. When something is done.
- **Vision.** Seeing what could be. What should be. What the world needs.
- **Care.** Putting time, attention, love into the work. Not because you have to, because you want to.
- **Context.** Understanding the human problems the work serves. The story it tells. The lives it touches.

AI can execute. It can't care. It can't choose. It can't see what should exist but doesn't yet.

That's still us.

That's still art.

---

## The Engineer's New Identity

If everyone can code, what makes an engineer?

Not writing syntax. **Systems thinking.** Understanding how pieces fit together. Seeing leverage points. Knowing what to build—and what not to.

The title "engineer" comes from "ingenuity." Cleverness. Finding elegant solutions. Building things that work.

That didn't go away. It just stopped hiding behind arcane knowledge.

Good engineering was never about knowing the most obscure APIs. It was about **solving problems beautifully**. That's harder now, not easier. Because the tool barrier is gone. What's left is pure craft.

The same is true for every discipline. What it means to be a designer, a writer, a musician—it's all being redefined.

And the answer is the same: **the work that remains is the human part.**

---

## Ownership Matters

There's one more thing.

If you're going to be an artist in the age of AI, you need to **own your tools.**

Not rent them. Own them.

When your work goes into a cloud service, gets trained on, your likeness taken without consent—you're not an artist. You're a data source.

Artists need sovereignty. Privacy. Control.

That's why decentralized systems matter. Why local AI matters. Why owning your stack—your tools, your data, your work—isn't a technical detail. It's a creative necessity.

You can't be an artist if you don't own your art.

---

## Our Best Nature

Creativity is what makes us human.

Not the tools. Not the techniques. The drive to make something that didn't exist before. To see a vision and bring it into the world.

That's **our best nature.**

And every generation gets new tools. Better tools. Tools that extend our reach, amplify our voice, let us do more with less.

The question is always the same: Will we use them well?

Will we adopt the tools of tomorrow and use them for good before anyone else can use them iniquitously?

That's what defines us. Not what we create. **Why we create it.**

---

## The Path Forward

Art will not go away because of AI.

It will give us seven-league boots. And we'll walk farther than we ever could before.

Some will walk towards beauty. Some towards truth. Some towards justice. Some just towards things that make them laugh.

Whatever kind of artisan you are—

**You can leverage technology to make the aspects of life you don't want to do go away. Or be minimized.**

**And you can spend more of your time making art. Doing the things you love.**

The time spent still determines the output. The care still matters. The vision still comes from you.

The boots just help you get there.

---

## A Personal Note

I don't know what it means to be an engineer anymore. Not in the old sense.

But I know what it means to build things that matter. To see a problem and create a solution. To take an idea and make it real.

That hasn't changed.

The tools collapsed. The craft remains.

And I'm going to keep walking.

Seven leagues at a time.
